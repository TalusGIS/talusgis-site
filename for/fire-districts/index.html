Who it's for

Fire protection districts

A fire district's GIS is a short list of things that must be right: every hydrant and its last flow test, every address, the station and zone boundaries, and the pre-plan for the buildings that matter. Talus Community holds all of it on one secure map that crews can read on a phone, with no per-seat license and no server farm.

What it does for you

Hydrants with their history. Each hydrant is a point with the fields you choose: main size, flow, color code, last test, last painted, owner. Inspections are edits with a full audit trail, so you know who recorded the test and when. Photos and the flow-test sheet attach to the hydrant. A hydrant from a water utility's database can be kept as a read-only, refreshed copy, so you never maintain two lists.

Pre-plans as attachments on the building. A target-hazard building is a polygon or a point; its pre-plan PDF, site photos, and Knox box location attach to the feature and open in the browser, in QGIS, and in ArcGIS Pro. Sharing is private by default and fails closed: a pre-plan shared to the operations group is visible to the operations group and to nobody else.

Addresses the district can search. Load your address points once and Talus geocodes against them internally, with no outside service and no credits, which also means it works when the internet does not. A county parcel or address service you cannot move stays referenced live from the catalog.

Zones, stations, and the public map. Response zones and station areas are polygons like any other layer. If the board wants residents to see hydrant locations or station boundaries, an administrator turns public sharing on and shares exactly those layers; everything else stays inside.

What you need

One machine inside your network. A virtual machine on the server the district already runs, or a small Linux host with Docker; four vCPUs and 8 GB are comfortable. The where it runs section describes each option. The stack also runs fully air-gapped if that is how your network is built: set map backgrounds to Downloaded, remove the public address search, configure no outbound connection, and it makes none.

Someone to run the install once. The runbook brings up the whole stack with one script, TLS included. Sign-in can use the district's existing identity provider or local accounts; multi-factor is built in, and the runbook shows how to require it, administrators first. Backups are written to a destination you name, encrypted to your own keys once you add them, and the restore drill is rehearsed, not assumed.

Your current data, exported once. Hydrants and zones from a county or a utility come across as Shapefile or GeoPackage; a spreadsheet with coordinates imports directly; KML from years of Google Earth comes in with its folders intact.

What it deliberately does not do

Talus is not dispatch and not CAD. It will not take a call, recommend a unit, or compute a response time; a computer-aided dispatch system does that, and Talus can hold a read-only copy of its tables if they live in a database Talus can reach. No routing: Talus will not draw the fastest route to a hydrant. No 3D, no utility network, no imagery analytics. No certifications yet: no CJIS, no StateRAMP, no SOC 2. Keep data that must sit behind such a certificate out of Talus until it has one.

Everything above is in Talus Community, free and AGPL. Talus+ adds support from the people who build it, dashboards and scheduled reports (hydrants overdue for testing, delivered to an inbox on a schedule), story maps, and Talus Field for offline collection in the truck.
