Who it's for

Conservancies, land trusts, and non-profits

A tree conservancy, a land trust, a watershed group, or a friends-of-the-park organization usually has better data than budget: a tree inventory in a spreadsheet, easement boundaries in a shapefile from the surveyor, trail GPS tracks from volunteers, and photos on three phones. Talus Community puts all of it on one map that the organization owns, for nothing, and keeps owning it.

What it does for you

A tree inventory that volunteers can keep current. Each tree is a point with the fields you define: species, diameter, condition, planting year, sponsor, last pruning. Coded-value domains keep the species list clean. Volunteers collect and photograph with QField on their own phones through a link; the crew credential is revoked from the portal when the planting season ends. Every edit records who and when.

Easements, preserves, and trails as layers. Boundaries from the surveyor import as Shapefile or GeoPackage; the easement deed and the baseline-documentation photos attach to the parcel they describe and open in the browser, QGIS, or ArcGIS Pro. Monitoring visits are edits with an audit trail, which is what a funder's annual report wants.

A public map and open data. Public sharing is off until you turn it on; then the trails and the public preserves become a map anyone opens without an account, and the Open Data portal publishes the datasets you choose, with the metadata that describes them and a DCAT-US feed.

Reporting without a consultant. Export any layer to CSV, GeoJSON, Shapefile, or GeoPackage for the grant report or the city partner. Talus+ adds scheduled PDF reports and dashboards for an organization that wants the numbers delivered.

What you need

One small server. A virtual machine on whatever the organization already runs, or a small cloud server with Docker; four vCPUs and 8 GB are comfortable for an organization this size. The where it runs section covers each option.

One technical volunteer or a small IT contractor, once. The install runbook brings the whole stack up with one script. Afterwards, backups run on a schedule to a destination you name and self-maintenance keeps the stack tidy; there is no separate utility to learn.

The data you have. Spreadsheets with coordinates, shapefiles from a surveyor or the county, KML and KMZ from years of Google Earth, GPX tracks converted once. Talus imports them with a preview before anything is written.

What it deliberately does not do

Talus is not a donor database, a CRM, or a volunteer scheduler, and it will not become one. It does not compute carbon, canopy, or ecosystem-service figures; export the inventory and run that in the tool built for it. No 3D, no routing, no imagery analytics. Anonymous public contributions are deliberately not accepted, and no public submission flow is offered.

Talus Community costs nothing and stays free under the AGPL. Using it obligates the organization to publish nothing. There is no per-seat license, no credit meter, and no license server that can turn it off.
