Who it's for

Rural counties with no GIS department

Many counties have GIS data and no GIS department: a parcel layer the assessor paid a contractor for, address points from the 911 project, roads from the state, an aerial from a few years ago, and one person in the clerk's or assessor's office who ends up responsible for all of it. Talus Community was validated against data at exactly that scale, and it runs on one machine.

What it does for you

County-scale data on one host. Talus was validated against a 308,000-polygon county parcel layer, over 300,000 address points, 35,000 road segments, and orthoimagery. Bulk import moves layers of that size through the normal doors on flat memory, with a dry-run preview and reprojection, so the parcel layer the contractor delivered goes in as it is. An aerial in GeoTIFF, JPEG 2000, or ERDAS IMG converts to tiles and serves from the same machine.

A public parcel viewer without a product. When public sharing is on, the parcel, address, and road layers you share to Public Access become a map residents and title companies open with no account, and the Open Data portal publishes the datasets you choose with ISO 19115 metadata and a DCAT-US feed the state's open-data harvester picks up automatically.

Staff in the departments they already use. The assessor opens the same layers in ArcGIS Pro, the road department in QGIS, through one connection file; every edit from any of them carries who and when. A field crew collects culverts, signs, or damage after a storm with QField on a phone, free.

The assessment system stays the system of record. If the CAMA or tax system keeps its data in SQL Server, PostgreSQL, MySQL, or MariaDB, a read-only copy of the table you choose refreshes nightly in Talus and joins the map; Talus never writes back.

What you need

One machine and one afternoon. A virtual machine on the county's existing Windows server under Hyper-V, or a Linux server with Docker. Four vCPUs and 8 GB are comfortable; at county scale four vCPUs carried roughly 150 named users at typical duty cycles, and adding CPU raises that ceiling. The where it runs section covers each option.

The data you already paid for. Parcels and roads as Shapefile or GeoPackage; address points as a CSV with coordinates or a shapefile; aerials as GeoTIFF, JPEG 2000, or ERDAS IMG. File geodatabases export from Pro to GeoPackage first, and every field comes across; attachments load with Pro's Append tool. Leading zeros in parcel IDs and FIPS codes are preserved on import.

Someone who can follow a runbook. The install, upgrade, and restore runbooks ship in the repository and are rehearsed before every release. Backups run on a schedule to a destination you name. Nothing phones home, and there is no license server.

What it deliberately does not do

Talus is not a parcel fabric. It holds parcel polygons and their attributes and serves them well; the legal-description and split-merge workflow stays in the tool that does that, and Talus imports the result. It is not a CAMA, tax, or recording system; those are integration points, read through a read-only copy. No routing, no 3D, no heavy imagery analytics. No certifications yet; a procurement checkbox that needs one cannot be satisfied by Talus today, and the FAQ says so plainly.

Talus Community is free, stays free under the AGPL, and obligates a county that runs it to publish nothing. Talus+ adds support from the people who build it, dashboards, scheduled reports, story maps, and Talus Field, on one public, flat price per deployment.
